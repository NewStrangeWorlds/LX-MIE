Conventions and units¶

This page summarises the definitions that are used by all three interfaces of LX-MIE. The underlying equations are given in Theory and numerical method.

Size parameter¶

The size parameter of a spherical particle with the radius \(r\) at the wavelength \(\lambda\) is

\[x = \frac{2 \pi r}{\lambda} \ .\]

It is dimensionless. The Mie efficiencies only depend on the size parameter and the refractive index. The single-particle functions of the Python package and the C++ library therefore take the size parameter as their argument, rather than the radius and the wavelength.

The wavelength refers to the medium surrounding the particle and the refractive index is the one of the particle relative to that medium. For particles in a gas, this is in practice the vacuum wavelength and the refractive index of the particle material.

Refractive index¶

The complex refractive index is given by its real part \(n\) and its imaginary part \(k\).

The sign of the imaginary part does not matter. Internally, LX-MIE uses the convention \(m = n - \mathrm{i} k\) and always converts the imaginary part to a negative value. Both sign conventions, \(n + \mathrm{i} k\) and \(n - \mathrm{i} k\), therefore give the same, physically correct result for an absorbing particle. The values of \(k\) from the files of the compilation can be used directly.

In the Python package, the refractive index is always passed as two separate real numbers (n_real, n_imag). The C++ library uses std::complex<double>.

Efficiencies and cross sections¶

LX-MIE calculates the extinction, scattering, and absorption efficiencies \(Q_\mathrm{ext}\), \(Q_\mathrm{sca}\), and \(Q_\mathrm{abs}\), with

\[Q_\mathrm{ext} = Q_\mathrm{sca} + Q_\mathrm{abs} \ .\]

The corresponding cross sections are obtained by multiplying the efficiencies with the geometric cross section of the particle:

\[C = Q \, \pi r^2 \ .\]

The absorption efficiency is calculated as the difference of the extinction and scattering efficiencies. For non-absorbing particles (\(k = 0\)), it can therefore deviate from zero by the numerical round-off error, including very small negative values.

Other frequently used quantities directly follow from the results, for example the single scattering albedo \(\omega_0 = Q_\mathrm{sca} / Q_\mathrm{ext}\).

Asymmetry parameter¶

The asymmetry parameter \(g\) is the mean cosine of the scattering angle,

\[g = \langle \cos\theta \rangle = \frac{1}{2} \int_{-1}^{1} p(\mu) \, \mu \, \mathrm{d}\mu \ ,\]

with \(\mu = \cos\theta\). It is \(g = 0\) for isotropic or symmetric scattering (e.g. Rayleigh scattering) and approaches \(g = 1\) for pure forward scattering.

Scattering angles¶

Wherever angles are passed to or returned by LX-MIE, they are given as the cosines of the scattering angles, \(\mu = \cos\theta\), and not as angles in degrees or radians. Forward scattering corresponds to \(\mu = 1\), backward scattering to \(\mu = -1\).

Phase function¶

The phase function \(p(\mu)\) for unpolarised incident light is normalised such that

\[\frac{1}{2} \int_{-1}^{1} p(\mu) \, \mathrm{d}\mu = 1 \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \int_{4\pi} p \, \mathrm{d}\Omega = 4 \pi \ .\]

Isotropic scattering therefore corresponds to \(p(\mu) = 1\).

Legendre series¶

The Legendre series coefficients \(\chi_l\) returned by LX-MIE are defined by

\[p(\mu) = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \left( 2l + 1 \right) \chi_l \, P_l(\mu) \ , \qquad \chi_l = \frac{1}{2} \int_{-1}^{1} p(\mu) \, P_l(\mu) \, \mathrm{d}\mu \ ,\]

where \(P_l\) are the Legendre polynomials. With the normalisation of the phase function given above, the first two coefficients are

\[\chi_0 = 1 \ , \qquad \chi_1 = g \ .\]

This is the convention used by, for example, the discrete ordinate radiative transfer code DISORT. Other codes include the factor \((2l+1)\) in the coefficients. In that case, the coefficients from LX-MIE have to be multiplied by \((2l+1)\).

The series of a particle with the size parameter \(x\) contains about \(2x\) non-negligible terms. Coefficients beyond the end of the series are returned as zero.

Units¶

Interface

Units

Python, C++: single particles

Only dimensionless quantities are used (size parameter, refractive index, efficiencies).

Python, C++: size distributions

Radii and wavelengths can be given in any length unit, as long as it is the same for both. Cross sections are returned in the square of that unit, e.g. in \(\mu\mathrm{m}\)2 for radii and wavelengths in \(\mu\mathrm{m}\).

Command-line tool

Radii and wavelengths in \(\mu\mathrm{m}\), cross sections in cm2.

Limitations¶

LX-MIE treats homogeneous, isotropic, spherical particles. Coated spheres, non-spherical particles, and magnetic materials are not supported. The phase function is the one for unpolarised incident light; the complex scattering amplitudes \(S_1\) and \(S_2\), from which all elements of the scattering matrix can be constructed, are available via lxmie.mie_amplitudes().
